Google Account Security Checkup: A 10-Minute Guide (Oct 2026)

By the NO SUS team · 12 October 2026 · 8 min read

The short answer

Open myaccount.google.com/security-checkup and fix anything flagged red or yellow. Then sign out devices you don't recognise, remove apps you no longer use, update your recovery phone and email, turn on 2-Step Verification, add a passkey on a phone only you use, and clear any Gmail forwarding or filters you didn't create. If you think you were hacked, change your password first and run the same checks.

Your Google account opens Gmail, Drive, Photos and every app where you tapped "Sign in with Google". If you have logged in on a college lab PC, a cyber cafe computer or a parent's phone, some of those sessions may still be open. This checklist follows Google's own help pages. Menu names are as of October 2026, when the main tab is called Security & sign-in.

Start with Google's Security Checkup

On your own phone or laptop, open myaccount.google.com/security-checkup, or click your profile picture and choose Recommended actions. Google flags items with a red, yellow or blue exclamation point by urgency, and shows a green shield when nothing needs attention. Fix red and yellow first. Forwarding and filters are set inside Gmail's own settings, so check them separately (below).

How do I see which devices are signed in to my Google account?

  1. Go to myaccount.google.com and select Security & sign-in.
  2. In the Your devices panel, select Manage all devices (or open google.com/devices).
  3. Open each entry. The list covers devices signed in now and ones used in the last few weeks.
  4. For anything you don't recognise or no longer use, select it and choose Sign out.
  5. If several sessions share one device name, sign out of all of them, as Google advises.

Look for exam-centre PCs, lab machines and phones you sold. The time shown is the last contact with Google, which background sync can make look more recent than your real use.

Back on Security & sign-in, find Recent security events and select Review security events. If something wasn't you, choose No, it wasn't me and follow the prompts, as Google's hacked-account guide recommends.

Remove third-party apps you no longer use

Quiz apps, resume builders and AI tools you signed into with Google may still be linked. Open myaccount.google.com/linkedapps, where Google sorts them into Sign in with Google, Linked account and Access to your Google Account, plus an Agent access filter for apps linked to Gemini or Google agents.

For each one you don't use, select See details, then Remove access (or Stop using Sign in with Google, or Delete link) and Confirm. Start with "Access to your Google Account", since those apps can read data from your account.

This matters because changing your password does not sign out some third-party apps. Stopping Sign in with Google doesn't delete your data inside that app.

Update your recovery phone and recovery email

Google uses these to get you back in and to warn you about suspicious activity. Go to Security & sign-in, then under How you sign in to Google select Recovery phone and Recovery email, and use Edit to fix anything outdated. Google's guidance:

  • The phone should receive SMS, belong only to you, and be one you carry. If a parent's number is still on the account from when it was set up, move it to your own SIM.
  • The recovery email should be one you check, and not the address you sign in with.
  • Changes can take up to 7 days, and codes may still go to your old details for 7 days. Do this before exam week, not during.

Two newer backups: recovery contacts, up to 10 trusted people who can help confirm it's you and never get access to your account (Google), and selfie for sign-in, a short selfie video announced in July 2026. Check eligibility for the selfie option at g.co/signin-selfie; Google hasn't listed which countries have it.

Turn on 2-Step Verification and pick a strong second step

  1. Go to myaccount.google.com and select Security & sign-in.
  2. Under How you sign in to Google, select Turn on 2-Step Verification (steps).
  3. Follow the prompts, then keep your 8-digit backup codes somewhere offline that only you can reach.

Google rates security keys as the strongest second step, then Google prompts, and steers you away from text-message codes because they are easier to phish. An authenticator app works without internet or mobile service, which helps in hostels with patchy coverage.

On a shared family phone, anyone who can unlock it can approve your Google prompts, since prompts go to Android phones signed in to your account. Remove your account from that phone, or use an authenticator app on one that's only yours.

Should I add a passkey to my Google account?

Yes, on a device only you use. A passkey signs you in with your fingerprint, face or screen-lock PIN. It can't be written down, copied or shared, which Google says makes it more resistant to phishing than a password. Google has offered them for Google Accounts since 2023 and promoted them again for Cybersecurity Awareness Month on 8 October 2026.

  1. Go to g.co/passkeys (or myaccount.google.com/signinoptions/passkeys).
  2. Select Create a passkey and confirm with your screen lock.

From Google's passkey help page:

  • Creating a passkey switches you to passkey-first sign-in. Your password still works; turn off Skip password when possible if you'd rather type it first.
  • Google advises against creating a passkey on a shared device, since anyone who knows its screen lock can get into your account. A family phone with a PIN everyone knows counts.
  • To remove one, go to Security & sign-in, then Passkeys and security keys, and select Remove.

Keep 2-Step Verification on too; Google says it helps if someone impersonates you and claims to have lost your passkey.

Check Gmail for forwarding and filters

Someone who gets in once can add a forwarding address or a filter and keep getting your mail after you change the password, because these are saved settings, not login sessions. Google's hacked-account guide lists them among the things to check.

On a computer, open Gmail, click the gear icon, then See all settings:

  1. Forwarding and POP/IMAP: if mail goes to an address you don't know, select Disable forwarding and Save Changes (Gmail Help). If a banner says you're forwarding your email and you never set it up, Google says to change your password right away.
  2. Filters and Blocked Addresses: look for any Forward to or Delete it filter you didn't create (Gmail security tips). A filter that quietly deletes mail from your bank or Google's security alerts is a red flag. Remove it with Delete (filter help).
  3. Accounts and Import: every address under Send mail as and Check mail from other accounts should be yours, and nobody unfamiliar should appear under Grant access to your account.

The Details link at the bottom right of your inbox lists the last 10 IP addresses that accessed your mail. Mail apps and mobile carriers can show as unfamiliar places.

Is Google Advanced Protection worth it?

For most students, no. For student journalists, activists, campaign volunteers or anyone being targeted, yes. The Advanced Protection Program is free. It requires a passkey or security key to sign in, lets only Google apps and verified third-party apps reach your account data, and adds extra checks on risky downloads.

The trade-offs, per Google's FAQ: some non-Google apps lose access, existing app passwords are revoked, and recovery takes a few days if you're locked out. With physical keys, Google recommends a primary plus at least one backup. Android has a separate, device-level Advanced Protection setting, and Google announced new Android 17 features for it on 1 October 2026.

Using Google on college lab PCs and shared family phones

On a device that isn't yours, Google recommends a private session. In Chrome, click the Profile icon, choose Open Guest profile, sign in there and close the Guest window when done. In other browsers, use a private window and sign out before closing.

Guest mode deletes history, cookies and site data on exit, but doesn't hide your activity from the device owner, the college network or the sites you visit. Don't use "Sign in to Chrome" on a lab PC: it is built to bring your bookmarks, passwords and more onto that machine. Afterwards, check Your devices from your phone. More in our guides to cyber cafe logins and logging out of WhatsApp Web.

For a shared family phone, decide whether your account needs to be on it. On Android, open Settings, then Passwords & accounts (or "Users & accounts" on some phones), pick your account and tap Remove account (Android Help). That deletes the account's email, contacts and settings from that phone.

What to do if you think your Google account was hacked

  1. If you can still sign in, change your password now. Google signs you out everywhere except devices you use to verify your identity, some third-party apps and smart home devices.
  2. If you're locked out, go to accounts.google.com/signin/recovery. Use a device, browser and place you normally sign in from, and answer every question, guessing if unsure (Google's tips). Wrong guesses don't end the process, and there's no limit on attempts.
  3. Run the checks above again, marking unfamiliar security events "No, it wasn't me" and undoing what an attacker would change: devices, recovery phone and email, 2-Step Verification methods, Gmail forwarding, filters, delegated access and linked apps.
  4. Check for money loss. Google suggests reviewing Google Pay, Google Play and Chrome's saved payment info. If money left your bank account or UPI, call your bank, then report it on the cyber crime helpline 1930 or at cybercrime.gov.in. Hacking without money loss can be filed there under Report Other Cybercrimes.
  5. Change other accounts that shared the password, remove Chrome extensions you don't recognise, and run trusted antivirus software on your computer, as Google advises.
  6. Ignore "Google support" phone numbers. Google says it won't help you sign in over the phone and warns against services that claim to.

Questions people ask

How do I see where my Google account is logged in?

Go to myaccount.google.com, select Security & sign-in, then Manage all devices in the Your devices panel (or open google.com/devices). Select any device you don't recognise and choose Sign out.

Does changing my Google password log me out of all devices?

Mostly. Google signs you out everywhere except devices you use to verify your identity, some third-party apps you've granted access, and smart home devices. Remove unwanted apps separately on the linked apps page.

Can a hacker still read my Gmail after I change my password?

Yes, if they set up automatic forwarding or a forwarding filter, because those are settings and stay in place. Check the Forwarding and POP/IMAP tab and the Filters and Blocked Addresses tab in Gmail's settings.

Should I create a Google passkey on a shared family phone?

No. Google advises against creating a passkey on a shared device, because anyone who knows that device's screen lock can get into your account. Create it on a phone or laptop that only you use.

Is Google Advanced Protection worth it for students?

Most students don't need it. It is free and built for people at high risk of targeted attacks, such as activists and political campaign staff, but it requires a passkey or security key, blocks some non-Google apps, and makes account recovery take a few days.
