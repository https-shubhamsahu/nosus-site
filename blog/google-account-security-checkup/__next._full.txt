<h2 id="start-with-googles-security-checkup">Start with Google&#39;s Security Checkup</h2>
<p>On your own phone or laptop, open myaccount.google.com/security-checkup, or click your profile picture and choose <strong>Recommended actions</strong>. <a href="https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/46526" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Google flags items</a> with a red, yellow or blue exclamation point by urgency, and shows a green shield when nothing needs attention. Fix red and yellow first. Forwarding and filters are set inside Gmail&#39;s own settings, so check them separately (below).</p>
<h2 id="how-do-i-see-which-devices-are-signed-in-to-my-google-account">How do I see which devices are signed in to my Google account?</h2>
<ol>
<li>Go to myaccount.google.com and select <strong>Security &amp; sign-in</strong>.</li>
<li>In the <strong>Your devices</strong> panel, select <strong>Manage all devices</strong> (or open google.com/devices).</li>
<li>Open each entry. The list covers devices signed in now and ones used in the last few weeks.</li>
<li>For anything you don&#39;t recognise or no longer use, select it and choose <strong>Sign out</strong>.</li>
<li>If several sessions share one device name, sign out of all of them, <a href="https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/3067630" rel="noopener" target="_blank">as Google advises</a>.</li>
</ol>
<p>Look for exam-centre PCs, lab machines and phones you sold. The time shown is the last contact with Google, which background sync can make look more recent than your real use.</p>
<p>Back on <strong>Security &amp; sign-in</strong>, find <strong>Recent security events</strong> and select <strong>Review security events</strong>. If something wasn&#39;t you, choose <strong>No, it wasn&#39;t me</strong> and follow the prompts, as <a href="https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/6294825" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Google&#39;s hacked-account guide</a> recommends.</p>
<h2 id="remove-third-party-apps-you-no-longer-use">Remove third-party apps you no longer use</h2>
<p>Quiz apps, resume builders and AI tools you signed into with Google may still be linked. Open <a href="https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/13533235" rel="noopener" target="_blank">myaccount.google.com/linkedapps</a>, where Google sorts them into <strong>Sign in with Google</strong>, <strong>Linked account</strong> and <strong>Access to your Google Account</strong>, plus an <strong>Agent access</strong> filter for apps linked to Gemini or Google agents.</p>
<p>For each one you don&#39;t use, select <strong>See details</strong>, then <strong>Remove access</strong> (or <strong>Stop using Sign in with Google</strong>, or <strong>Delete link</strong>) and <strong>Confirm</strong>. Start with &quot;Access to your Google Account&quot;, since those apps can read data from your account.</p>
<p>This matters because changing your password <a href="https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/41078" rel="noopener" target="_blank">does not sign out</a> some third-party apps. Stopping Sign in with Google doesn&#39;t delete your data inside that app.</p>
<h2 id="update-your-recovery-phone-and-recovery-email">Update your recovery phone and recovery email</h2>
<p>Google uses these to get you back in and to warn you about suspicious activity. Go to <strong>Security &amp; sign-in</strong>, then under <strong>How you sign in to Google</strong> select <strong>Recovery phone</strong> and <strong>Recovery email</strong>, and use <strong>Edit</strong> to fix anything outdated. <a href="https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/183723" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Google&#39;s guidance</a>:</p>
<ul>
<li>The phone should receive SMS, belong only to you, and be one you carry. If a parent&#39;s number is still on the account from when it was set up, move it to your own SIM.</li>
<li>The recovery email should be one you check, and not the address you sign in with.</li>
<li>Changes can take up to 7 days, and codes may still go to your old details for 7 days. Do this before exam week, not during.</li>
</ul>
<p>Two newer backups: <strong>recovery contacts</strong>, up to 10 trusted people who can help confirm it&#39;s you and never get access to your account (<a href="https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/technology/safety-security/world-password-day-2026/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Google</a>), and <strong>selfie for sign-in</strong>, a short selfie video <a href="https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/technology/safety-security/selfie-video-sign-in/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">announced in July 2026</a>. Check eligibility for the selfie option at g.co/signin-selfie; Google hasn&#39;t listed which countries have it.</p>
<h2 id="turn-on-2-step-verification-and-pick-a-strong-second-step">Turn on 2-Step Verification and pick a strong second step</h2>
<ol data-checklist>
<li>Go to myaccount.google.com and select <strong>Security &amp; sign-in</strong>.</li>
<li>Under <strong>How you sign in to Google</strong>, select <strong>Turn on 2-Step Verification</strong> (<a href="https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/185839" rel="noopener" target="_blank">steps</a>).</li>
<li>Follow the prompts, then keep your 8-digit backup codes somewhere offline that only you can reach.</li>
</ol>
<p>Google rates <a href="https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/46526" rel="noopener" target="_blank">security keys as the strongest</a> second step, then Google prompts, and steers you away from text-message codes because they are easier to phish. An <a href="https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/185839" rel="noopener" target="_blank">authenticator app works</a> without internet or mobile service, which helps in hostels with patchy coverage.</p>
<p>On a shared family phone, anyone who can unlock it can approve your Google prompts, since prompts go to Android phones signed in to your account. Remove your account from that phone, or use an authenticator app on one that&#39;s only yours.</p>
<h2 id="should-i-add-a-passkey-to-my-google-account">Should I add a passkey to my Google account?</h2>
<p>Yes, on a device only you use. A passkey signs you in with your fingerprint, face or screen-lock PIN. It can&#39;t be written down, copied or shared, which Google says makes it <a href="https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/13548313" rel="noopener" target="_blank">more resistant to phishing</a> than a password. Google has offered them for Google Accounts <a href="https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/technology/safety-security/world-password-day-2026/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">since 2023</a> and promoted them again for Cybersecurity Awareness Month on <a href="https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/technology/safety-security/how-to-set-up-passkey/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">8 October 2026</a>.</p>
<ol>
<li>Go to g.co/passkeys (or myaccount.google.com/signinoptions/passkeys).</li>
<li>Select <strong>Create a passkey</strong> and confirm with your screen lock.</li>
</ol>
<p>From <a href="https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/13548313" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Google&#39;s passkey help page</a>:</p>
<ul>
<li>Creating a passkey switches you to passkey-first sign-in. Your password still works; turn off <strong>Skip password when possible</strong> if you&#39;d rather type it first.</li>
<li>Google advises against creating a passkey on a shared device, since anyone who knows its screen lock can get into your account. A family phone with a PIN everyone knows counts.</li>
<li>To remove one, go to <strong>Security &amp; sign-in</strong>, then <strong>Passkeys and security keys</strong>, and select <strong>Remove</strong>.</li>
</ul>
<p>Keep 2-Step Verification on too; <a href="https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/technology/safety-security/world-password-day-2026/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Google says</a> it helps if someone impersonates you and claims to have lost your passkey.</p>
<h2 id="check-gmail-for-forwarding-and-filters">Check Gmail for forwarding and filters</h2>
<p>Someone who gets in once can add a forwarding address or a filter and keep getting your mail after you change the password, because these are saved settings, not login sessions. <a href="https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/6294825" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Google&#39;s hacked-account guide</a> lists them among the things to check.</p>
<p>On a computer, open Gmail, click the gear icon, then <strong>See all settings</strong>:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Forwarding and POP/IMAP</strong>: if mail goes to an address you don&#39;t know, select <strong>Disable forwarding</strong> and <strong>Save Changes</strong> (<a href="https://support.google.com/mail/answer/10957" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Gmail Help</a>). If a banner says you&#39;re forwarding your email and you never set it up, Google says to change your password right away.</li>
<li><strong>Filters and Blocked Addresses</strong>: look for any <strong>Forward to</strong> or <strong>Delete it</strong> filter you didn&#39;t create (<a href="https://support.google.com/mail/answer/7036019" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Gmail security tips</a>). A filter that quietly deletes mail from your bank or Google&#39;s security alerts is a red flag. Remove it with <strong>Delete</strong> (<a href="https://support.google.com/mail/answer/6579" rel="noopener" target="_blank">filter help</a>).</li>
<li><strong>Accounts and Import</strong>: every address under <strong>Send mail as</strong> and <strong>Check mail from other accounts</strong> should be yours, and nobody unfamiliar should appear under <strong>Grant access to your account</strong>.</li>
</ol>
<p>The <strong>Details</strong> link at the bottom right of your inbox lists the <a href="https://support.google.com/mail/answer/45938" rel="noopener" target="_blank">last 10 IP addresses</a> that accessed your mail. Mail apps and mobile carriers can show as unfamiliar places.</p>
<h2 id="is-google-advanced-protection-worth-it">Is Google Advanced Protection worth it?</h2>
<p>For most students, no. For student journalists, activists, campaign volunteers or anyone being targeted, yes. The <a href="https://landing.google.com/advancedprotection/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Advanced Protection Program</a> is free. It requires a passkey or security key to sign in, lets only Google apps and verified third-party apps reach your account data, and adds extra checks on risky downloads.</p>
<p>The trade-offs, per <a href="https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/7539956" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Google&#39;s FAQ</a>: some non-Google apps lose access, existing app passwords are revoked, and recovery takes a few days if you&#39;re locked out. With physical keys, Google recommends a primary plus at least one backup. Android has a separate, device-level Advanced Protection setting, and Google announced <a href="https://blog.google/security/android-advanced-protection-updates/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">new Android 17 features</a> for it on 1 October 2026.</p>
<h2 id="using-google-on-college-lab-pcs-and-shared-family-phones">Using Google on college lab PCs and shared family phones</h2>
<p>On <a href="https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/2917834" rel="noopener" target="_blank">a device that isn&#39;t yours</a>, Google recommends a private session. In Chrome, click the <strong>Profile</strong> icon, choose <strong>Open Guest profile</strong>, sign in there and close the Guest window when done. In other browsers, use a private window and sign out before closing.</p>
<p>Guest mode deletes history, cookies and site data on exit, but <a href="https://support.google.com/chrome/answer/6130773" rel="noopener" target="_blank">doesn&#39;t hide your activity</a> from the device owner, the college network or the sites you visit. Don&#39;t use &quot;Sign in to Chrome&quot; on a lab PC: it is built to bring your <a href="https://support.google.com/chrome/answer/185277" rel="noopener" target="_blank">bookmarks, passwords and more</a> onto that machine. Afterwards, check <strong>Your devices</strong> from your phone. More in our guides to <a href="/blog/is-it-safe-to-log-in-on-a-cyber-cafe-computer/">cyber cafe logins</a> and <a href="/blog/logout-whatsapp-web-from-another-computer/">logging out of WhatsApp Web</a>.</p>
<p>For a shared family phone, decide whether your account needs to be on it. On Android, open <strong>Settings</strong>, then <strong>Passwords &amp; accounts</strong> (or &quot;Users &amp; accounts&quot; on some phones), pick your account and tap <strong>Remove account</strong> (<a href="https://support.google.com/android/answer/7664951" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Android Help</a>). That deletes the account&#39;s email, contacts and settings from that phone.</p>
<h2 id="where-no-sus-fits">Where NO SUS fits</h2>
<p>If you only need a file on a lab or print-shop computer, you can avoid signing Google in there at all. With <a href="/guides/#chat">NO SUS Chat</a>, you open your NO SUS domain (like riya.nosus.foo) on that computer, scan its QR code with the NO SUS Android app, match the number and approve with your phone&#39;s screen lock. The computer never receives your Google password or Google token, and it shows only your NO SUS Chat, for the time you pick: 15 minutes, 1 hour or 8 hours. The limit: anything you download or print can stay on that computer, so delete it before you leave (see our <a href="/blog/print-shop-document-privacy/">print shop privacy guide</a>).</p>
<h2 id="what-to-do-if-you-think-your-google-account-was-hacked">What to do if you think your Google account was hacked</h2>
<ol data-checklist>
<li><strong>If you can still sign in, change your password now.</strong> Google <a href="https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/41078" rel="noopener" target="_blank">signs you out everywhere</a> except devices you use to verify your identity, some third-party apps and smart home devices.</li>
