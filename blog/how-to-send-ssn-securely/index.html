How to Send Your SSN Securely (and Why Not by Email or Text)

The short answer

Don't type your Social Security number into a plain email or text message. The safest ways are the requester's own secure portal, a phone call to a number you looked up yourself, or handing it over in person. If it has to go in writing, use an end-to-end encrypted chat or a link that opens once, keep it out of any subject line, and delete the message afterwards.

Your SSN never expires, so don't send it anywhere it can sit for years. That rules out the two ways most people send it: a normal email and a text message. Here is what to do instead, and what to do if it's already out.

Is it safe to send your SSN over email?

No, not in a normal email. The IRS says plainly that "standard email is not encrypted", and the FTC tells businesses that regular email "is not a secure method for sending sensitive data" and to caution staff against emailing Social Security numbers. Gmail encrypts mail while it travels when the other provider supports it (TLS), but Google notes that TLS does not protect email while it is stored on a server. Your number then sits in your Sent folder and in their inbox, in every backup, for as long as nobody deletes it.

If you must use email, keep the number out of the subject line (the IRS points out that even encrypted email doesn't encrypt the subject) and out of the body. Put a one-time link in the email instead.

Is it safe to text your SSN?

Not by SMS. Apple and Google both say SMS and MMS messages are not end-to-end encrypted. iMessage is, and so are RCS chats in Google Messages when both people use Google Messages with RCS chats on and a lock shows. Even then, the number stays on both phones and in their backups, so ask the other person to delete it once they've copied it, and delete your own copy.

For the IRS itself, use your IRS online account, or the Document Upload Tool when a notice or letter asks for documents, not plain email.

Who actually needs your SSN?

Before you send it anywhere, do what SSA suggests: ask why the number is needed, how it will be used, and what happens if you don't provide it. A real employer, bank or tax preparer will have an answer. The SSA's inspector general adds one simple rule: don't share your SSN with anyone who contacts you first, however routine the request sounds.

How to spot an SSN scam

  • Social Security will never ask for sensitive information through social media, email or text message.
  • Real SSA staff never threaten to suspend your SSN, and never demand gift cards, crypto, wire transfers or cash (SSA OIG).
  • The IRS doesn't make first contact by email or social media. A letter or notice comes first.
  • The IRS will never ask for your Identity Protection PIN. Calls, emails or texts asking for it are scams.

Already sent your SSN by email or text? What to do now

  1. Ask the recipient to delete the message, then delete yours from Sent and Trash.
  2. Freeze your credit at Equifax, Experian and TransUnion. The FTC says a freeze costs nothing to place or lift, doesn't affect your score, and lasts until you lift it. You have to contact all three.
  3. Or add a fraud alert instead: it lasts one year and can be renewed, and the bureau you contact tells the other two. An extended alert lasts seven years but needs an FTC identity theft report or a police report.
  4. Get an IRS Identity Protection PIN. Anyone with an SSN or ITIN who can verify their identity is eligible, and a new PIN is generated each year (IRS).
  5. With a myE-Verify account, turn on Self Lock, which helps stop anyone else using your SSN for an E-Verify job check. Unlock it before you start a job with an employer that uses E-Verify.
  6. Check your credit reports. All three bureaus now let you check each report once a week for free, permanently, through AnnualCreditReport.com.
  7. If you see a loan, card or job you don't recognise, report it at IdentityTheft.gov for a recovery plan (FTC).

Don't count on a new number. The FTC says you can't get a new Social Security number without evidence that someone is already misusing yours.

Questions people ask

How do I send my SSN securely over email?

Don't put the number in a normal email's body or subject line: the IRS says standard email is not encrypted. It also stays in both mailboxes. Use the requester's secure portal instead, or email a one-time link that holds the number and opens once.

Is it safe to text your Social Security number?

Not by SMS or MMS, which Apple and Google both say are not end-to-end encrypted. iMessage and RCS chats that show a lock are end-to-end encrypted, but the number still sits on both phones, so delete it once it has been received.

What is the best way to send a Social Security number?

Type it into the requester's own secure portal, read it out on a call to a number you looked up yourself, or give it in person. If none of those work, send a link that opens once and tell the person by phone that it is coming.

Does Social Security or the IRS ask for your SSN by email or text?

No. SSA says it will never ask for sensitive or personal information through social media, email or text message, and the IRS says its first contact is a letter or notice, not an email.

What should I do if I sent my SSN by email by mistake?

Ask the recipient to delete it, delete your own copy, then freeze your credit at Equifax, Experian and TransUnion. A freeze is free and lasts until you lift it. An IRS Identity Protection PIN and an E-Verify Self Lock add two more locks.

Can I get a new Social Security number if mine was exposed?

Usually not. The FTC says you can't get a new Social Security number without evidence that someone else is misusing your number, so locking and monitoring are the realistic steps.
