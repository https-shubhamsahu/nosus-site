1e:T25f9,<h2 id="what-did-sbi-warn-about-on-10-october-2026">What did SBI warn about on 10 October 2026?</h2>
<p>State Bank of India used <a href="https://x.com/TheOfficialSBI/status/2108897841703764321" rel="noopener" target="_blank">a post on X</a> on 10 October 2026 to warn customers about the &quot;jumped deposit&quot; scam. The post explains that scammers pretend a small credit landed in your account by accident, as a way to gain your trust. It ran under SBI&#39;s National Cyber Security Month and &quot;Special Campaign 6.0&quot; labels. On 11 October the warning was picked up by <a href="https://thelivenagpur.com/2026/10/11/sbi-warns-of-jumped-deposit-upi-scam-that-could-drain-bank-accounts/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">The Live Nagpur</a>, <a href="https://the420.in/sbi-jumped-deposit-scam-upi-fraud-warning/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">The420</a>, <a href="https://www.news4hackers.com/sbi-upi-fraud-warning-fake-refund-requests-unexpected-deposits/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">News4Hackers</a> and the Kannada outlet <a href="https://suddione.com/sbi-warns-against-new-upi-jumped-deposit-scam-that-drains-accounts/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Suddione</a>.</p>
<p>This is not the first time. SBI had warned about the same scam earlier in the year, and <a href="https://www.business-standard.com/finance/personal-finance/unexpected-money-in-your-account-sbi-warns-of-jumped-deposit-scam-126060300851_1.html" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Business Standard</a> explained how it works on 3 June 2026.</p>
<p>None of the reports cited here give figures for money lost or people affected, so treat any viral number with suspicion.</p>
<h2 id="how-does-the-jumped-deposit-scam-work">How does the jumped deposit scam work?</h2>
<p>Based on SBI&#39;s description as reported by <a href="https://www.business-standard.com/finance/personal-finance/unexpected-money-in-your-account-sbi-warns-of-jumped-deposit-scam-126060300851_1.html" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Business Standard</a> and <a href="https://suddione.com/sbi-warns-against-new-upi-jumped-deposit-scam-that-drains-accounts/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Suddione</a>, it runs in four steps:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>The bait.</strong> A stranger sends a small amount to your account, often a few rupees or a few hundred.</li>
<li><strong>The call.</strong> Someone calls or messages to say they sent it by mistake. The tone can be friendly or panicked, and they want the money back immediately.</li>
<li><strong>The switch.</strong> Instead of asking you to send the money yourself, they send you something to approve. SBI&#39;s post calls it a high-value payment request; Business Standard and Suddione describe it as a UPI &quot;collect&quot; request. NPCI stopped person-to-person collect requests from 1 October 2025 (<a href="https://www.theweek.in/wire-updates/business/2025/08/14/dcm65-biz-npci-collect.html" rel="noopener" target="_blank">PTI via The Week</a>), so today the prompt is more likely to come from an account registered as a merchant, or as a QR code or payment link. Either way, it is for far more than the original credit.</li>
<li><strong>The PIN.</strong> You think you are returning their money, so you enter your UPI PIN without reading the screen. The larger amount leaves your account.</li>
</ol>
<p>The urgency is the point. It stops you checking what you are approving. As Business Standard points out, the scam usually doesn&#39;t need your password or OTP, because you approve the payment yourself.</p>
<p>The request might not always look the same. The RBI&#39;s fraud awareness booklet, <a href="https://rbidocs.rbi.org.in/rdocs/content/pdfs/BEAWARE07032022.pdf" rel="noopener" target="_blank">BE(A)WARE</a>, describes the same trick in other settings: a &quot;request money&quot; prompt sent to online sellers, and QR codes people are told to scan to receive money. Its rule covers every version: &quot;there is no need to enter PIN / password anywhere to receive money.&quot;</p>
<h2 id="why-students-and-young-professionals-are-exposed">Why students and young professionals are exposed</h2>
<p><strong>Your phone number is your payment address.</strong> On Google Pay, your <a href="https://support.google.com/pay/india/answer/11428814?hl=en" rel="noopener" target="_blank">UPI Number</a> can be your mobile number, and once it is linked, anyone who types that number in can pay you. That number already sits in class WhatsApp groups, hostel lists, resumes, internship forms and rental agreements. Anyone who has it can send you ₹1 and then call.</p>
<p><strong>Your name may come with it.</strong> If Google Pay&#39;s &quot;Allow people to find you&quot; setting is on, people with your number can see your name and photo (<a href="https://support.google.com/pay/india/answer/11623446?hl=en" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Google Pay Help</a>). A caller who greets you by name sounds more believable.</p>
<p><strong>It works on honest people.</strong> The pitch relies on the instinct to return money that isn&#39;t yours. Careful people fall for it because they are trying to do the right thing.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>What to do in the next 5 minutes</strong></p>
<p>If someone is calling you about money &quot;sent by mistake&quot; right now:</p>
<ol>
<li>Don&#39;t enter your UPI PIN, scan a QR code or approve any request they send.</li>
<li>Hang up. Open your bank&#39;s own app and check the balance.</li>
<li>If you already approved something, call your bank&#39;s official number and <strong>1930</strong> immediately, then file at <a href="https://cybercrime.gov.in/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">cybercrime.gov.in</a>.</li>
</ol>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="money-credited-by-mistake-what-you-should-do">Money credited by mistake? What you should do</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Treat any PIN prompt as a payment.</strong> The <a href="https://rbidocs.rbi.org.in/rdocs/content/pdfs/BEAWARE07032022.pdf" rel="noopener" target="_blank">RBI booklet</a> says that if an app wants your PIN to complete a transaction, you are sending money, not receiving it. The same goes for scanning a QR code.</li>
<li><strong>Check the balance, not the message.</strong> Open your bank&#39;s app or statement. An SMS or a screenshot the caller sends you proves nothing; the RBI notes that fraudsters send spoofed SMSes made to look like your bank. If no money actually arrived and you &quot;refund&quot; it, you have simply paid a stranger.</li>
<li><strong>Don&#39;t refund anyone who calls.</strong> SBI&#39;s advice is to verify unexpected credits through the bank&#39;s official app, website or customer care before returning anything (<a href="https://thelivenagpur.com/2026/10/11/sbi-warns-of-jumped-deposit-upi-scam-that-could-drain-bank-accounts/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">The Live Nagpur</a>). If a wrong credit is real, tell your bank and let it handle the reversal. Passing money on to a stranger&#39;s account can carry another risk: the <a href="https://rbidocs.rbi.org.in/rdocs/content/pdfs/BEAWARE07032022.pdf" rel="noopener" target="_blank">RBI booklet</a> warns that people whose accounts are used to move money along a chain (money mules) can become the target of police investigation.</li>
<li><strong>Never approve a request you didn&#39;t start.</strong> Read the amount and the payee name every time. If the caller asks you to install a screen-sharing app &quot;to help&quot;, refuse; the RBI booklet describes screen-sharing apps as one way fraudsters take over payment apps.</li>
<li><strong>Block and report.</strong> Block the number and tell your bank. Report the call, SMS or WhatsApp message on <a href="https://sancharsaathi.gov.in/sfc/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Chakshu</a> in the Sanchar Saathi portal. If you have already lost money, Chakshu itself directs you to call 1930 or file at cybercrime.gov.in. Business Standard notes that fast reporting improves the chance of stopping further losses.</li>
<li><strong>Know what a real bank call looks like, and still guard your PIN.</strong> SBI says its genuine calls come from numbers starting with 1600 (<a href="https://www.business-standard.com/finance/personal-finance/unexpected-money-in-your-account-sbi-warns-of-jumped-deposit-scam-126060300851_1.html" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Business Standard</a>). Under TRAI&#39;s rules, the 1600xx and 1601xx series are designated for service and transactional calls (<a href="https://pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2311989" rel="noopener" target="_blank">PIB, 18 September 2026</a>). A 1600 number tells you who is likely calling. It doesn&#39;t make every request safe, and no call needs your UPI PIN or OTP. To check a bank&#39;s official numbers, use Sanchar Saathi&#39;s <a href="https://sancharsaathi.gov.in/FinancialInstitutions/fiHome.jsp" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Trusted Contact Details</a> lookup.</li>
</ol>
<h2 id="where-no-sus-fits">Where NO SUS fits</h2>
<p>NO SUS has nothing to do with payments and can&#39;t block this scam. The overlap is exposure: on UPI your phone number can work as your payment handle, so every public group or form that holds it lets a stranger credit you and then call. The guide to <a href="/blog/receive-files-without-sharing-your-phone-number">receiving files without sharing your phone number</a> covers several ways to cut that down. One is NO SUS <a href="/guides#drop">Drop</a>, a personal address where people send you a file of up to 25 MB from a browser, with no account and no phone number exchanged. It won&#39;t hide a number that&#39;s already out there. Freelancers can apply the same idea to <a href="/blog/how-to-collect-documents-from-clients-securely">collecting documents from clients</a>.</p>
