DPDP Act Checklist for Small Businesses in India (2026)

By Shubham Sahu, founder of NO SUS1 November 2026, 7 min read

The short answer

Any business that keeps personal data of customers, staff or candidates in digital form is a Data Fiduciary under India's DPDP Act, whatever its size; there is no turnover or headcount threshold and no small-business exemption has been notified. The main duties (notice, security safeguards, breach reporting, erasure, a published contact person) and the penalties start in mid-May 2027, 18 months after the Rules were published on 13 November 2025. A small business can get ready in 12 steps: map the data, cut what it doesn't need, write a notice, lock down access, keep logs, sign vendor contracts, set deletion dates and write a breach plan.

India's data protection law covers far more businesses than most owners think. If you keep customer phone numbers in Excel, staff Aadhaar scans in a Drive folder or client PAN cards in WhatsApp, the Digital Personal Data Protection Act applies to you. Its main duties start in mid-May 2027. That sounds far away, but the work below takes a few weekends, and big clients are already asking their vendors about it.

This is a practical checklist, not legal advice. Notice and contract wording is worth getting checked by a lawyer. For trade-specific versions, see the checklists for HR, payroll and staffing firms and for CA firms.

Does the DPDP Act apply to my business?

Almost certainly, if you handle anyone's personal data for work.

  • You are a Data Fiduciary if you decide why and how personal data is used (Act, section 2(i)). A "person" includes an individual, a proprietor, a firm and a company (section 2(s)).
  • There is no size threshold. The Act and Rules set no turnover, headcount or record-count limit for ordinary businesses. A 10-person office with employee Aadhaar scans is covered.
  • Digital means more than apps. The Act covers personal data collected digitally, and paper records that were later scanned, photographed or typed in (section 3(a)). A register that never touches a computer is outside it, but almost nothing stays on paper.
  • What is left out: personal or domestic use, and data the person made public themselves (section 3(c)).
  • No small-business exemption yet. Section 17(3) lets the government exempt notified classes, such as DPIIT-recognised startups, from notice, accuracy, erasure and access duties. No such notification had been issued as of 1 November 2026, and the power can never remove the security and breach-reporting duties.

Key dates

The DPDP Rules, 2025 were published on 13 November 2025 with a phased start (Rule 1; G.S.R. 843(E)).

The DPDP checklist for small businesses

Tick these off as you go. Each one points to the part of the law it comes from.

  1. Make a data map. List every kind of personal data you hold (customers, staff, candidates, vendors), where each copy sits (WhatsApp, Gmail, Drive, Tally, laptops, phones, printouts) and who can open it. You can't protect or delete what you can't find.
  2. Cut what you don't need. Consent covers only data necessary for the stated purpose (section 6(1)). If a masked Aadhaar or the last four digits of an account do the job, stop collecting the full number. Our free Aadhaar copy masker runs in the browser.
  3. Pick the ground for each use. For each item on the map, write down whether you rely on consent or on a legitimate use in section 7: data a person gave you voluntarily for something they asked for, employment purposes, a legal duty, a court order or an emergency.
  4. Write a plain notice. Wherever you ask for consent, give a standalone notice: an itemised list of the data, the specific purpose, and how to withdraw consent, use their rights and complain to the Board (Rule 3). Offer it in English or any of the 22 scheduled languages the person picks (section 5(3)).
  5. Make withdrawal as easy as saying yes. If consent was one tap, withdrawal can't need a letter (section 6(4)). Keep a record of who agreed to what and when, because you have to prove it if challenged (section 6(10)).
  6. Publish a contact person. Put the name or role and the email of the person who answers data questions on your website or app (Rule 9), with your grievance response time, which can be no more than 90 days (Rule 14(3)). The Rules don't say how a business with no website meets this, so a one-page site is cheap insurance.
  7. Lock down access. Rule 6 lists the minimum: encryption, masking or tokens; access control; logs and monitoring that can catch unauthorised access; and backups. In practice that means one account per person, two-step verification everywhere, shared folders instead of personal WhatsApp, and removing access the day someone leaves.
  8. Keep logs for a year. Rule 6(1)(e) and Rule 8(3) ask you to keep access logs and the personal data for at least one year from processing, then erase them unless another law needs them kept. Check that your email, Drive or software actually records who opened what.
  9. Sign contracts with your vendors. Anyone who processes data for you (payroll software, a cloud CRM, an outsourced accountant, a background check agency) needs a valid contract with security terms (section 8(2), Rule 6(1)(f)). You stay responsible for what they do (section 8(1)).
  10. Set deletion dates. Erase data once the purpose is over or consent is withdrawn, unless a law requires you to keep it (section 8(7)), and make your vendors erase their copies too. Write one retention period per document type, put a recurring reminder in the calendar and note when you deleted.
  11. Write a one-page breach plan. A breach includes accidental sharing, like a salary sheet sent to the wrong group (section 2(u)). There is no minimum size. You must tell each affected person and the Board without delay, and send the Board a detailed report within 72 hours (Rule 7). Decide now who does what, and keep a message template ready.
  12. Check for children's data. Anyone under 18 is a child. You need verifiable consent from a parent before processing a child's data, and no tracking or targeted ads aimed at children (section 9, Rule 10). Clinics and schools have narrow exemptions in the Fourth Schedule of the Rules.

What happens if you don't

The Act's Schedule sets the maximum penalties:

BreachUp to
No reasonable security safeguards (section 8(5))₹250 crore
Not reporting a breach to the Board or the people affected (section 8(6))₹200 crore
Children's data duties (section 9)₹200 crore
Anything else in the Act or Rules₹50 crore

These are ceilings, not price tags. The Board can fine only after an inquiry and a hearing, and only for a significant breach. It must weigh the gravity and duration, the type of data, repeat offences, any gain made, how fast and well you limited the damage, and the impact of the fine on your business (section 33). A business can also offer a voluntary undertaking, which ends proceedings on that matter if the Board accepts it (section 32). As of November 2026 the Board had no members appointed, and its power to inquire and fine starts only in May 2027 (PIB).

The more immediate cost is commercial. Larger clients send vendors privacy and security questionnaires, and a vendor who can't answer them loses the contract long before any regulator calls.

What a small business can skip

  • Significant Data Fiduciary duties. A Data Protection Officer, independent audits and impact assessments apply only to businesses the government notifies as Significant Data Fiduciaries. None can be notified before May 2027, and the criteria point at large or high-risk processing (section 10).
  • Becoming a Consent Manager. That is a registered business for managing other people's consents, open only to Indian companies with ₹2 crore net worth.
  • The three-year deletion rule. Rule 8(1) and its 48-hour warning apply only to e-commerce and social media platforms with 2 crore users and gaming platforms with 50 lakh.

If you collect Aadhaar copies

Aadhaar has its own rules on top of the DPDP Act, and they apply to paper too. Under the Aadhaar (Sharing of Information) Regulations, anyone collecting an Aadhaar number or a document containing one must tell the person the purpose and whether it is mandatory, get their consent, never display it publicly, keep it secure, and not keep it longer than the purpose needs (regulations 5 and 6). A masked copy that shows only the last four digits is often enough; see masked Aadhaar vs photocopy.

Questions people ask

Does the DPDP Act apply to small businesses?

Yes. The Act applies to any person who decides why and how personal data is processed, and its definition of a person includes individuals, firms and companies. It has no turnover, headcount or record-count threshold for ordinary businesses. It covers personal data in digital form, including paper records that were scanned or typed into a computer.

When does the DPDP Act start for businesses?

The business duties and the penalty provisions start 18 months after the DPDP Rules were published on 13 November 2025, which is 13 May 2027 on a plain count. Until then only the Data Protection Board's setup rules are in force, and section 43A of the IT Act still applies.

Is there a DPDP exemption for startups or MSMEs?

Not yet. Section 17(3) lets the government exempt notified classes, including DPIIT-recognised startups, from duties such as notice and erasure, but no such notification had been issued as of 1 November 2026. Even if one comes, the Act does not allow it to remove the duty to keep data secure or to report breaches.

What is the penalty for a DPDP breach?

The Act sets maximums: up to ₹250 crore for failing to take reasonable security safeguards, ₹200 crore for not reporting a breach, ₹200 crore for children's data duties and ₹50 crore for other breaches. The Data Protection Board can fine only after an inquiry, and must weigh the gravity of the breach, the type of data, how quickly the business acted and the impact on the business.

Do I need a Data Protection Officer?

Only if the government notifies you as a Significant Data Fiduciary, which none can be before May 2027 and which is aimed at large or high-risk processing. A small business must instead publish the contact details of a person who can answer questions about how it handles personal data.

Do I need consent for every piece of personal data?

No. Section 7 lists legitimate uses that need no consent, including data a person gives you voluntarily for a purpose they asked for, employment purposes, legal duties and medical emergencies. Security, breach reporting and erasure duties apply either way.
