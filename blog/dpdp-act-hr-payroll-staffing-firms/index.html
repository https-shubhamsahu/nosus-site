DPDP Act for HR, Payroll and Staffing Firms: An Employee Data Checklist

By Shubham Sahu, founder of NO SUS1 November 2026, 5 min read

The short answer

Under India's DPDP Act an employer may process employee data for employment purposes without consent (section 7(i)), but security safeguards, breach reporting, erasure and a published contact person still apply from mid-May 2027. Candidates are not yet employees, so give them a notice and collect only what hiring needs. A payroll provider working on a client's instructions is usually that client's Data Processor and needs a contract with security terms; a staffing firm that is the legal employer is generally a Data Fiduciary for its associates' statutory data. Write the roles into every contract.

No small business holds more sensitive personal data per person than an HR, payroll or staffing firm. One joining kit has an Aadhaar, a PAN, a bank account, a UAN, a salary, an address, often a medical form and a family nominee. Multiply that by every associate on your rolls, and a single salary sheet in the wrong WhatsApp group becomes a reportable breach under India's DPDP Act.

The general duties are in our DPDP Act checklist for small businesses. This page covers what is different for employee and candidate data. It is practical guidance, not legal advice.

When the duties start

The DPDP Rules were published on 13 November 2025. The duties below, and the Data Protection Board's power to fine, start 18 months later: mid-May 2027, or 13 May 2027 on a plain count (G.S.R. 843(E)). There is no size threshold and no exemption for small firms has been notified.

Employees: consent is not the main ground

Section 7(i) of the Act lets an employer process personal data "for the purposes of employment" or to protect itself from loss or liability, such as preventing corporate espionage or protecting trade secrets, and to provide a benefit the employee asked for. Payroll, attendance, PF and ESI filings, appraisals and exit formalities sit comfortably here.

What that does not remove:

  • Security safeguards (section 8(5), Rule 6): encryption or masking, access control, logs kept for a year, backups.
  • Breach reporting (section 8(6), Rule 7): tell the affected people and the Board without delay, with a detailed report to the Board within 72 hours.
  • Erasure (section 8(7)): delete when the purpose ends, unless a law requires you to keep the record.
  • A published contact person and a grievance process (Rule 9, Rule 14(3)).

Some law firms read the Act as saying that no consent notice is needed for data processed on the employment ground, because section 5 ties the notice to consent requests. Neither MeitY nor the Board has confirmed that. A short privacy note in the offer letter or HR policy costs little and covers you either way.

Candidates are not employees yet

The Act is silent on whether shortlisting, interviews and background checks count as "purposes of employment". Law firms are split (King Stubb & Kasiva). The cautious route:

  • Collect only what the hiring step needs. A CV and contact details for shortlisting; ID and bank details only at offer stage.
  • Before a background check, give the candidate a notice that says what will be checked, from which sources and why, and get their consent.
  • When a candidate isn't hired, the purpose is over. Delete their documents after the one-year minimum in Rule 8(3), unless a law needs them kept.

Fiduciary or processor? It depends on the data set

The Act puts the main duties on the Data Fiduciary, the party that decides why and how data is used, and lets it use a Data Processor only under a valid contract (section 8(2)). The fiduciary stays liable for what the processor does (section 8(1)).

The staffing case is the least settled. Commentators disagree on how the Act applies when two employers share one worker (Bar & Bench). The practical answer is the same everywhere: write each party's role, for each data set, into the contract.

The HR data checklist

  1. Map every HR data set. Candidates, employees, ex-employees, associates at clients, nominees and dependants. For each, note where copies sit: HRMS, payroll software, Excel, email, recruiters' phones, printed joining kits.
  2. Write down the ground for each. Employment use (section 7(i)) for employees; consent with a notice for candidates and background checks; legal duty for statutory filings.
  3. Stop collecting what payroll doesn't need. Full bank statements, full Aadhaar numbers and medical history are rarely required. A masked Aadhaar and a cancelled cheque usually do.
  4. Move documents off personal phones. Recruiters' WhatsApp is the biggest leak in most firms. Give candidates one upload route instead (how to collect documents securely).
  5. Lock down access by role. Salary data visible to payroll only, documents to HR only, and access removed the day someone leaves. Turn on two-step verification and check that your systems keep access logs for a year (Rule 6).
  6. Put roles into every contract. Client agreements, vendor agreements and background check agency terms should say who is fiduciary and who is processor for which data, require security safeguards, and require deletion at the end.
  7. Set retention periods. Labour, tax and PF laws require some records to be kept; keep those for the period the law sets and no longer. Everything else goes when its purpose ends, after Rule 8(3)'s one-year minimum.
  8. Plan for exits. When someone leaves, keep what the law and final settlement need, revoke their system access, and schedule the rest for deletion.
  9. Write the breach plan for HR's usual mistakes. A payslip to the wrong person, a salary sheet in a group, a lost laptop. Each one triggers Rule 7's notices. Name who calls the employee, who emails the Board and who writes the 72-hour report.
  10. Publish the contact person. Name the role and email that answers employees' and candidates' data questions, on the website and in the HR policy (Rule 9).

Aadhaar copies in joining kits

Collecting employee Aadhaar copies is not banned, but the UIDAI regulations set conditions that already apply, on paper and on screen: tell the person the purpose and whether giving Aadhaar is mandatory, get consent, never display it, keep it secure and don't keep it longer than needed (regulations 5 and 6). The Aadhaar Data Vault rule applies to entities that use UIDAI authentication or e-KYC, not to an employer that only keeps copies (UIDAI ADV FAQ).

Questions people ask

Do employers need employee consent under the DPDP Act?

Not for employment purposes. Section 7(i) allows processing for the purposes of employment or to protect the employer from loss or liability without consent. The employer still has to keep the data secure, report breaches, erase it when the purpose ends unless a law requires keeping it, and publish a contact person.

Is a payroll company a data fiduciary or a data processor?

When a payroll provider runs payroll on a client's instructions, the client usually decides why and how the data is used, so the client is the Data Fiduciary and the provider is its Data Processor. The client must have a valid contract with security terms (section 8(2), Rule 6) and stays responsible for the provider's work. The same provider is a Data Fiduciary for its own employees' data.

Who is the data fiduciary for contract staff, the staffing firm or the client?

The Act does not say directly. A staffing firm that is the legal employer of its associates decides how their payroll, PF and tax data is used, so it is generally treated as a Data Fiduciary for that data, while the client may be a fiduciary for data it collects itself, such as site access logs. Commentators disagree on the edges, so the contract should state each party's role for each data set.

Do I need consent for employee background verification?

It is unsettled. Section 7(i) covers employment purposes, but the Act does not say whether checks on a candidate who is not yet an employee fall under it. The cautious route is a clear notice to the candidate on what will be checked, from which sources and why, with their consent, and collecting only what the check needs.

How long can HR keep data of candidates who weren't hired?

Only as long as the hiring purpose needs, unless a law requires longer. Rule 8(3) separately asks every business to keep personal data and processing logs for at least one year from processing, so set a retention period that covers that year and then delete.
