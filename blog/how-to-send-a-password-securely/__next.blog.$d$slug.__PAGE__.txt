1e:T340a,<p>A password pasted into a chat leaves your screen in seconds but stays everywhere else: your chat history, the other person&#39;s, every device either of you is logged in on, and any chat backup either of you makes. Email is the same, with a copy in your Sent folder and one in their inbox, often for years. At work it&#39;s worse. On paid Slack plans, messages are kept <a href="https://slack.com/help/articles/203457187-Customize-data-retention-in-Slack" rel="noopener" target="_blank">for the lifetime of the workspace by default</a>.</p>
<p>GitGuardian&#39;s 2026 secrets report found that <a href="https://thehackernews.com/2026/03/the-state-of-secrets-sprawl-2026-9.html" rel="noopener" target="_blank">28% of leak incidents in 2025 started entirely outside source code</a>, in tools like Slack, Jira and Confluence, and that those leaks were more often rated critical than leaks in code. The same report found that 64% of secrets confirmed valid in 2022 still worked four years later. Old secrets don&#39;t expire on their own.</p>
<p>The fix is simple: send the secret in a way that doesn&#39;t leave a permanent copy, and change it if it ever did.</p>
<h2 id="which-method-should-you-use">Which method should you use?</h2>
<div class="table-scroll" role="region" aria-label="Table" tabindex="0"><table><thead><tr><th scope="col">What you&#39;re sharing</th><th scope="col">Best option</th><th scope="col">Why</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><th scope="row">A login the other person needs for months (family OTT account, shared college portal)</th><td>Password manager sharing</td><td>They get their own copy, no chat trail</td></tr><tr><th scope="row">A one-off password for a colleague or friend</th><td>One-time secret link, password sent separately</td><td>The link dies after one view</td></tr><tr><th scope="row">Your home Wi-Fi for a guest</th><td>QR code or iPhone password sharing</td><td>Nothing is typed or sent</td></tr><tr><th scope="row">An API key for a developer or freelancer</th><td>A new, limited key sent by one-time link</td><td>You can revoke it without breaking anything else</td></tr><tr><th scope="row">Your OTP or UPI PIN</th><td>Nothing. Never send it</td><td>No genuine person needs it</td></tr></tbody></table></div>
<h2 id="option-1-share-it-through-a-password-manager">Option 1: Share it through a password manager</h2>
<p>If someone needs a login for the long term, a password manager is the cleanest route because the password moves straight from your vault to theirs.</p>
<p><strong>Google Password Manager</strong> lets you share a saved password, but <a href="https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/95606?hl=en&co=GENIE.Platform%3DDesktop" rel="noopener" target="_blank">only with members of your Google family group</a>. On a computer:</p>
<ol>
<li>Open Chrome and go to More, then Passwords and autofill, then Google Password Manager.</li>
<li>Under Passwords, select the one you want to share.</li>
<li>Select Share, pick the family member, then Share and Done.</li>
</ol>
<p>The password is saved into their Google Account and fills in automatically. Google&#39;s own advice: to stop sharing, change the password, and never share one you reuse elsewhere, because the other person may then get into your other accounts too.</p>
<p><strong>1Password</strong> can <a href="https://support.1password.com/share-items/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">share an item with anyone, even people without a 1Password account</a>, either to anyone holding the link or only to specific email addresses (the recipient then verifies their email). As of October 2026, its <a href="https://www.1password.dev/sdks/share-items" rel="noopener" target="_blank">developer documentation</a> lists expiry options of one hour, one day, seven days, 14 days or 30 days, plus a one-view setting. The item is encrypted on your device and <a href="https://support.1password.com/share-items-security/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">the share secret is never sent to 1Password&#39;s servers</a>. One catch: if you later edit the item, the old link still shows the old version, so share again after changes.</p>
<h2 id="option-2-send-a-one-time-secret-link">Option 2: Send a one-time secret link</h2>
<p>For a password, Wi-Fi key or code that someone needs once, a one-time link is better than any chat message. You paste the secret into a tool, it gives you a link, and the link stops working after it&#39;s opened or after a time limit.</p>
<p><strong>Bitwarden Send</strong> is a good free option. <a href="https://bitwarden.com/help/create-send/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">All Bitwarden users can create a text Send</a> (file Sends need a paid plan), and <a href="https://bitwarden.com/help/receive-send/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">anyone with the link can open it</a>, including people with no Bitwarden account. A text Send holds <a href="https://bitwarden.com/help/about-send/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">up to 1,000 encrypted characters</a>. When you create one you can:</p>
<ol>
<li>Set a deletion date (as of October 2026, <a href="https://bitwarden.com/help/create-send/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">the default is seven days and the maximum is 30</a>).</li>
<li>Use Limit views to cap how many times it opens. Set it to 1.</li>
<li>Add a password the recipient must enter.</li>
<li>Hide the text by default, so it doesn&#39;t show on screen until they tap to reveal it.</li>
<li>Hide your email address from the recipient.</li>
</ol>
<p><strong>Yopass</strong> is an open-source alternative that <a href="https://github.com/jhaals/yopass" rel="noopener" target="_blank">encrypts the secret in your browser and deletes one-time secrets after the first view</a>. Its own README recommends self-hosting an instance if you share sensitive information, which makes it a good fit for a college tech team or a small company with someone who can run a server.</p>
<p>Whichever tool you use, keep the expiry short (an hour or a day, not a week) and ask the recipient to confirm once they&#39;ve opened it. If they say the link was already used when they clicked it, assume someone else saw the secret and change it. For more tools side by side, see our <a href="/blog/self-destructing-note-tools-compared">comparison of self-destructing note tools</a>.</p>
<h2 id="what-does-the-key-is-in-the-url-fragment-mean">What does &quot;the key is in the URL fragment&quot; mean?</h2>
<p>Good one-time tools encrypt the secret before it leaves your device, then put the decryption key in the link itself, after the <code>#</code> sign. A Bitwarden Send link carries both the Send&#39;s ID and its encryption key after the <code>#</code>, and Bitwarden says <a href="https://bitwarden.com/help/send-encryption/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">the key is never included in a network request</a>.</p>
<p>That works because of how browsers handle links. Everything after <code>#</code> is called the fragment, and according to MDN, &quot;The fragment is not sent to the server when the URI is requested&quot; (<a href="https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/URI/Reference/Fragment" rel="noopener" target="_blank">MDN</a>). The browser keeps that part to itself. So the server stores a locked box, and only the person holding the full link has the key.</p>
<p>Two things follow from this:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>The link is the secret</strong> until it&#39;s opened. Anyone who sees the full link first can open it, which is why short expiry and one view matter.</li>
<li><strong>A Send password is a gate, not the lock.</strong> Bitwarden&#39;s documentation says the optional password <a href="https://bitwarden.com/help/send-encryption/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">controls access but isn&#39;t part of the encryption</a>. It still helps, especially with the next trick.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="split-channel-delivery-link-one-way-password-another">Split-channel delivery: link one way, password another</h2>
<p>Split-channel means the secret never travels in one piece. Send the one-time link by email, and say the link&#39;s password on a phone call or send it by SMS. Someone who gets into one account or one phone only gets half.</p>
<p>A practical version for a team: post nothing in the Slack channel except &quot;sent you a link by email&quot;, then share the password for that link on a quick call. 1Password&#39;s email-restricted links give you a similar effect automatically, because the recipient has to verify their email address before the item opens.</p>
<h2 id="when-saying-it-out-loud-is-the-best-option">When saying it out loud is the best option</h2>
<p>For short secrets, a phone call or telling someone in person leaves no copy at all. Just avoid speakerphone in a crowded place.</p>
<p>Wi-Fi is even easier, because you don&#39;t need to say anything:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Android:</strong> open Settings, then Network and internet, then Internet, pick your network and tap Share. <a href="https://support.google.com/android/answer/9075847?hl=en" rel="noopener" target="_blank">A QR code appears</a> that a guest scans to join. Menu names vary a little between phone brands.</li>
<li><strong>iPhone:</strong> when a guest&#39;s Apple device tries to join your network, your iPhone (awake, past the lock screen and on that network) shows a Share Password prompt. It <a href="https://support.apple.com/en-us/102635" rel="noopener" target="_blank">needs Wi-Fi and Bluetooth on and each person saved in the other&#39;s contacts</a>.</li>
</ul>
<p>If your router supports a guest network, give visitors that instead of your main password.</p>
<h2 id="how-to-share-an-api-key-or-bank-details">How to share an API key or bank details</h2>
<p><strong>API keys</strong> deserve their own rules, because anyone who finds a key can use it on your account and your bill. GitGuardian counted about <a href="https://blog.gitguardian.com/the-state-of-secrets-sprawl-2026-pr/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">29 million secrets exposed on public GitHub in 2025</a>, and leaks of AI-service keys rose 81% in a year.</p>
<ol>
<li>Create a separate key for that person or project, never your main one.</li>
<li>Give it the smallest permissions and spending limits your provider allows.</li>
<li>Send it through a one-time link, not Slack, Jira, email or a GitHub issue.</li>
<li>Revoke it the day the work ends.</li>
</ol>
<p><strong>Bank details</strong> for receiving money (account number and IFSC) go only to the person paying you; the <a href="https://www.rbi.org.in/scripts/BS_PressReleaseDisplay.aspx?prid=53185" rel="noopener" target="_blank">RBI&#39;s fraud advisory</a> puts account numbers first in its list of details not to share. If someone you pay regularly messages that their bank account has &quot;changed&quot;, call them on a number you already have before sending money.</p>
<h2 id="what-you-should-never-send-to-anyone">What you should never send to anyone</h2>
<p>The RBI&#39;s advice is blunt: never share your <a href="https://www.rbi.org.in/scripts/BS_PressReleaseDisplay.aspx?prid=53185" rel="noopener" target="_blank">login ID, password, PIN, UPI PIN, OTP or card details with anyone, not even bank officials</a>. In practice:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>OTPs for banking, UPI, logins or account changes.</strong> Read the full SMS before reading any code aloud. If it says the code is for a login, payment or account change, it&#39;s not for anyone else.</li>
<li><strong>Your UPI PIN.</strong> The same RBI advisory says <a href="https://www.rbi.org.in/scripts/BS_PressReleaseDisplay.aspx?prid=53185" rel="noopener" target="_blank">receiving money never requires scanning a QR code or entering a PIN</a>. A &quot;collect request&quot; asking for your PIN to receive money is a scam.</li>
<li><strong>Net-banking passwords, card numbers with CVV, and 2FA backup codes.</strong></li>
<li><strong>Remote access.</strong> The RBI lists remote-access apps as a common fraud route and advises against downloading any unknown app, since it can secretly read your data. If a caller asks you to install one, hang up.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="already-pasted-a-password-in-a-chat">Already pasted a password in a chat?</h2>
<p>Delete the message for everyone if the app still lets you, then change the password anyway, because you can&#39;t know who has already seen it or which backups hold it. Our guide to <a href="/blog/do-disappearing-messages-really-disappear">whether disappearing messages really disappear</a> explains why deleting isn&#39;t enough on its own. If it was your Google password, run through a full <a href="/blog/google-account-security-checkup">Google account security checkup</a> too.</p>
<h2 id="where-no-sus-fits">Where NO SUS fits</h2>
<p><a href="/#try">NO SUS Burn Notes</a> work like the one-time links above, with no account needed for you or the recipient. The note is encrypted in your browser, the direct link keeps the key after the <code>#</code>, it opens once and is then gone, and you choose when it expires. Two limits to know. Each single note also gets a two-digit pairing code, and while that code is valid (20 minutes by default) the note&#39;s key is held on the server too, then swept. And once someone reads a note, they can copy or photograph it; no browser can stop that. Setup steps are in the <a href="/guides#burn">Burn guide</a>.</p>
