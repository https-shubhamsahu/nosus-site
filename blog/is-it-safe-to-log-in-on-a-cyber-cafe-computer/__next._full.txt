<p>Not every login carries the same risk. Here is a rough guide for a normal cafe, college lab or print-shop PC you know nothing about:</p>
<div class="table-scroll" role="region" aria-label="Table" tabindex="0"><table><thead><tr><th scope="col">What you&#39;re logging into</th><th scope="col">On a cafe or lab PC</th><th scope="col">Why</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><th scope="row">Net banking, payments, trading</th><td>Avoid</td><td><a href="https://ibanking.pnb.bank.in/web/L001/webpages/advisory.html" rel="noopener" target="_blank">PNB tells customers</a> to avoid internet banking from cyber cafes and shared computers</td></tr><tr><th scope="row">Gmail or Google account</th><td>Okay with the checklist below</td><td>2-Step Verification and remote sign-out limit the damage</td></tr><tr><th scope="row">DigiLocker</th><td>Prefer the phone app</td><td>It holds identity documents, and their downloads are easy to leave behind</td></tr><tr><th scope="row">College portal, exam or job form</th><td>Usually okay with the checklist</td><td>Change the password later if you reuse it anywhere</td></tr></tbody></table></div>
<h2 id="what-can-go-wrong-when-you-log-in-on-a-public-computer">What can go wrong when you log in on a public computer</h2>
<p><strong>Someone records what you type.</strong> A keylogger is software or a small device that records your keystrokes. Software keyloggers often arrive bundled with other malware. Hardware ones usually need someone with physical access to plug in a gadget, such as a USB device or a fake connector between the keyboard cable and the PC, <a href="https://www.kaspersky.com/blog/keylogger/1573/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">according to Kaspersky</a>. More capable versions also grab <a href="https://www.veracode.com/security/keylogger" rel="noopener" target="_blank">periodic screenshots and anything you copy and paste</a>.</p>
<p>This is not theoretical. In 2011, staff at two public libraries on the southern edge of Greater Manchester found <a href="https://www.theregister.com/2011/02/15/hardware_keyloggers_manchester_libraries/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">USB keyloggers plugged into keyboard sockets on the back of their PCs</a>, most likely to capture banking logins. Those PCs already had filtering software and accounts that stopped users installing programs. A plug-in keylogger needs no installing, so neither helped.</p>
<p><strong>The browser remembers you for the next person.</strong> When you type a new password, Chrome <a href="https://support.google.com/chrome/answer/95606?hl=en" rel="noopener" target="_blank">offers to save it</a>. Google&#39;s 2-Step Verification screen has a &quot;Don&#39;t ask again on this computer&quot; box that Google says to tick <a href="https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/185839?hl=en" rel="noopener" target="_blank">only on devices you regularly use and don&#39;t share</a>. Some sites add their own &quot;Remember me&quot; or &quot;Stay signed in&quot; checkbox. Accept any of these on a cafe PC and the next customer may walk straight into your account.</p>
<p><strong>You sign in to the browser instead of the website.</strong> Signing in to Gmail is different from signing in to Chrome itself. Signing in to Chrome is how you <a href="https://support.google.com/chrome/answer/185277?hl=en" rel="noopener" target="_blank">get your bookmarks, passwords and more on all your devices</a>. On a machine strangers use, that is the opposite of what you want.</p>
<p><strong>Files stay behind.</strong> Private browsing does not clean up downloads. Google&#39;s own help page says <a href="https://support.google.com/chrome/answer/9845881?hl=en" rel="noopener" target="_blank">downloaded files stay on your device until you delete them</a>, even in Incognito. The marksheet, Aadhaar copy or offer letter you downloaded to print is still sitting in the Downloads folder after you leave.</p>
<p><strong>Someone else can see or control the machine.</strong> Remote-access tools like AnyDesk exist to <a href="https://support.anydesk.com/docs/abuse-management.md" rel="noopener" target="_blank">let someone reach and control a computer from somewhere else</a>. Cafe management software is sold with features to <a href="https://antamedia.com/cafe/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">manage computers remotely</a> and track every session in detailed logs. None of this is sinister by default, but the person running the cafe may see more than you assume.</p>
<p><strong>The cafe keeps records by law.</strong> India&#39;s <a href="https://www.wipo.int/wipolex/en/text/494915" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Cyber Cafe Rules, 2011</a> require a cafe to verify your ID and keep a copy for at least a year, log your login and logout times, and back up the history of websites accessed on its computers for at least a year. The same rules say screens outside cubicles must face the open area of the cafe, so the person standing behind you can often read your screen.</p>
<p><strong>Private windows only tidy up the browser.</strong> Guest and Incognito windows mainly stop the browser keeping your history, cookies and site data after you close them. Google says Guest mode <a href="https://support.google.com/chrome/answer/6130773?hl=en" rel="noopener" target="_blank">doesn&#39;t hide your activity</a> from the device owner, the network operator or your school. Neither mode does anything about a keylogger that is already on the machine.</p>
<h2 id="how-to-judge-a-cyber-cafe-or-lab-computer-before-you-log-in">How to judge a cyber cafe or lab computer before you log in</h2>
<p>Do these quick checks before you type anything:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Look at the keyboard plug.</strong> Follow the keyboard cable to the computer. A small extra adapter between the cable and the port is a red flag. Ask for another machine.</li>
<li><strong>Check the taskbar.</strong> Icons for remote-access tools such as AnyDesk or TeamViewer in the corner of the screen suggest the machine is set up to be reached from elsewhere.</li>
<li><strong>See what the last person left.</strong> If the browser opens on someone else&#39;s inbox or a half-filled form, the cafe does not reset machines between customers. Hong Kong&#39;s government InfoSec site suggests <a href="https://www.infosec.gov.hk/en/best-practices/person/tips-on-using-public-computer" rel="noopener" target="_blank">picking places that wipe and reinstall the system for each user</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Try opening a Guest window.</strong> If Guest mode and private windows are blocked, Google advises <a href="https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/2917834?hl=en" rel="noopener" target="_blank">clearing cache, cookies and history both before you sign in and after you sign out</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Pick your seat.</strong> Choose a screen nobody can stand behind.</li>
</ul>
<p>None of these checks will find well-hidden malware. They only rule out the obvious problems.</p>
<h2 id="before-during-and-after-a-checklist-for-logging-in-safely">Before, during and after: a checklist for logging in safely</h2>
<h3 id="before-you-sit-down">Before you sit down</h3>
<ol data-checklist>
<li>Ask whether it has to happen on this computer. Much of it can be done on your phone (see the next section).</li>
<li>Turn on 2-Step Verification for your Google account if it is off. It exists to protect you <a href="https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/185839?hl=en" rel="noopener" target="_blank">in case your password is stolen</a>, which is exactly the cyber cafe risk. Our <a href="/blog/google-account-security-checkup/">Google account security checkup guide</a> walks through it.</li>
</ol>
<h3 id="while-youre-logged-in">While you&#39;re logged in</h3>
<ol data-checklist style="counter-reset: b-step 2" start="3">
<li>Open a clean window. In Chrome, click the profile icon at the top right and choose <strong>Open Guest profile</strong>. Google recommends <a href="https://support.google.com/chrome/answer/6130773?hl=en" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Guest mode for public computers</a> such as a library or cafe. In Edge or Firefox, <a href="https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/2917834?hl=en" rel="noopener" target="_blank">open a private window</a>.</li>
<li>Type the website address yourself instead of clicking links or bookmarks already on the machine.</li>
<li>Sign in to the website only. If the browser offers to turn on sync or sign you in to Chrome, decline.</li>
<li>Refuse every &quot;save password&quot;, &quot;remember me&quot; or &quot;stay signed in&quot; prompt, and leave &quot;Don&#39;t ask again on this computer&quot; unticked.</li>
<li>If a bank login truly cannot wait, use the bank&#39;s on-screen keyboard. <a href="https://ibanking.pnb.bank.in/web/L001/webpages/advisory.html" rel="noopener" target="_blank">PNB recommends it</a> for entering passwords, and Kaspersky notes it stops malware <a href="https://www.kaspersky.com/blog/keylogger/1573/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">reading what you type on the physical keyboard</a>. It does not help against tools that capture the screen.</li>
<li>Never walk away with a session open, even to collect a printout from the counter.</li>
</ol>
<h3 id="after-youre-done">After you&#39;re done</h3>
<ol data-checklist style="counter-reset: b-step 8" start="9">
<li>Sign out of every site. Delete whatever you downloaded or scanned, and check the Recycle Bin too.</li>
<li>Close every Guest or private window. Closing the Guest window deletes <a href="https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/2917834?hl=en" rel="noopener" target="_blank">browsing history, cookies and site data</a>.</li>
<li>On your phone, open your Google Account and go to <strong>Security &amp; sign-in</strong>, then <strong>Your devices</strong>, then <strong>Manage all devices</strong> (labels as of October 2026; <a href="https://support.google.com/mail/answer/8154?hl=en" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Gmail Help</a> also links straight to this <a href="https://myaccount.google.com/device-activity" rel="noopener" target="_blank">device list</a>). Select the cafe computer and tap <strong>Sign out</strong>. One computer can show up as several sessions, so Google says to <a href="https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/3067630?hl=en" rel="noopener" target="_blank">sign out of every session with that device&#39;s name</a>. If you used WhatsApp Web, unlink that too: see <a href="/blog/logout-whatsapp-web-from-another-computer/">how to log out of WhatsApp Web from another computer</a>.</li>
<li>If you typed a password you also use anywhere else, change it from your own device. Hong Kong&#39;s InfoSec site gives <a href="https://www.infosec.gov.hk/en/best-practices/person/tips-on-using-public-computer" rel="noopener" target="_blank">the same advice</a>.</li>
</ol>
<h2 id="safer-ways-to-get-it-done-without-typing-your-password">Safer ways to get it done without typing your password</h2>
<p><strong>Use your phone.</strong> DigiLocker has <a href="https://www.digilocker.gov.in/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Android and iPhone apps</a>, and so do most banks. If the only reason you are at the computer is to print, the shop has its own privacy risks: see <a href="/blog/print-shop-document-privacy/">how to keep documents private at a print shop</a>, and use a masked Aadhaar where it is accepted (<a href="/blog/how-to-share-aadhaar-copy-safely/">how to share an Aadhaar copy safely</a>).</p>
<p><strong>Sign in with a passkey from your phone.</strong> On the Google sign-in page, choose <strong>Try another way</strong>, then <strong>Use your passkey</strong>, and scan the QR code with your phone (<a href="https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/13548313?hl=en" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Google&#39;s steps</a>). Google says <a href="https://security.googleblog.com/2023/05/so-long-passwords-thanks-for-all-phish.html" rel="noopener" target="_blank">neither the passkey nor your screen lock information is sent to the computer</a>, so there is no password for a keylogger to catch. Three caveats:</p>
<ul>
<li>The computer checks that your phone is nearby over Bluetooth, so this only works on a machine with Bluetooth turned on.</li>
<li>The login still creates a <a href="https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/3067630?hl=en" rel="noopener" target="_blank">new session</a> on that computer, so do the remote sign-out step anyway.</li>
<li>If the computer offers to create a passkey on itself, say no. Google warns <a href="https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/13548313?hl=en" rel="noopener" target="_blank">not to create one on a shared device</a>, because anyone who can get past that device&#39;s screen lock could then sign in as you.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="where-no-sus-fits">Where NO SUS fits</h2>
<p>If what you need on the borrowed computer is a document, not your whole Gmail, NO SUS Chat is built for that. Open your NO SUS domain (like <code>riya.nosus.foo</code>) on the cafe or lab computer, scan its QR code with the NO SUS Android app, match the number, and approve with your phone&#39;s screen lock. The computer never receives your Google password or Google token. It shows only your NO SUS Chat, for at most an hour. The limits are real: it needs the Android app, anything you download or print can stay on that computer, and it cannot stop a compromised machine from capturing what is on screen. The <a href="/guides/#chat">NO SUS Chat guide</a> has the details.</p>
